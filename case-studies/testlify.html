THE REAL CHALLENGE

BUILDING STRUCTURE WHERE NONE EXISTED

Most case studies document how a designer built a feature. This one is about something harder: deciding what to build, why, and what could break — before a single frame was opened.

Founders provided high-level references: “Build something like this competitor feature.” No PRDs. No structured documentation. Requirements arrived through Slack, sales calls, and client tickets — vague, half-defined, and missing edge cases.

The core complexity: One employer toggle could ripple simultaneously into candidate status across multiple assessments, PDF output structure, ATS sync behaviour, shareable link access controls, historical data consistency, and cross-platform differences across web, iOS, and Android. Nothing existed in isolation.

My actual responsibility

This wasn't designing flows. It was acting as the product's decision layer — applying a 5-Why discipline to every brief before opening Figma:

Surface tension My decision responsibility
Ambiguous requirements Convert "make resume screening automatic" into structured logic, edge cases, validation rules, and failure states — with documented trade-offs founders could act on
Cascading dependencies Map downstream effects before designing: what does this toggle break in ATS sync, PDF generation, or white-label config?
Technical feasibility Validate with backend teams on constraints before promising solutions to stakeholders
Competing stakeholder priorities Mediate between founders, sales, and CS — prevent design-by-committee with documented trade-offs and clear rationale
Quality gates Block releases until UX matched spec, cross-browser issues were resolved, and logic was sound — regardless of delivery pressure
What Made This Different from Previous Roles

In my earlier 4 years as UX/UI Designer across service and product companies (including EdTech at Eduauraa and MT Educare/Zee), I received clearer specifications and worked on simpler, screen-based solutions. Testlify challenged me in three ways:

1. Velocity vs. Quality
Fast-paced startup where I had to maintain design quality while matching aggressive timelines. Initially struggled — made mistakes, shipped work I wasn’t proud of. Over time, built momentum and systems to deliver quality at speed.
2. Multi-touchpoint Complexity
Unlike previous single-platform work, every Testlify feature affected employer portal, candidate portal, white-label environments, reseller configurations, PDF reports, and 14 international languages including Arabic and Chinese. One toggle could impact candidate status, report generation, ATS sync, and cross-platform behaviour simultaneously.
3. Ambiguous Requirements
Founders provided high-level direction (“build resume screening”), not detailed specs. I learned to structure undefined requirements myself — sometimes independently, sometimes seeking clarity from my reporting manager or founders in scrum calls.

HOW I WORKED

Design principles that guided 505 tickets across 1.7 years:

1. Control Over Automation

AI should augment human judgment, not replace it. In Resume Parser, I designed manual override controls even though AI accuracy was high — hiring decisions are too high-stakes for full automation.

2. Transparency in Complexity

When systems make decisions (AI scoring, test logic, candidate filtering), show users why and how. Example: Resume Parser displays criteria-level scoring breakdown, not just final scores.

3. Progressive Disclosure

Reveal complexity gradually. Resume Parser flow: Step 1 (simple job selection) → Step 2 (AI-assisted criteria) → Step 3 (advanced thresholds for power users).

4. Guardrails, Not Gates

Enable power users while protecting novices. Confirmation modals, default limits, clear warnings — but don’t block capabilities.

5. Consistency Across Chaos

Maintained patterns across rapid expansion: status indicators, empty states, error handling, action buttons, form validation.

HUMAN JUDGMENT IN AI-DRIVEN PRODUCTS

Working on an AI hiring platform gave me direct experience of where AI genuinely helps — and where it fails in ways that hurt real people. I was deliberate about this line every day.

AI Handled
  • Edge-case stress-testing and logic gap identification
  • Suggesting candidate-matching criteria for screening rules
  • Resume parsing and structured data extraction
  • Confidence scoring on resume-to-role fit
  • Surface-level, repeatable, time-consuming tasks
Human Judgment Required
  • Resolving founder vs. sales conflicts — requires empathy and political awareness no model has
  • Designing irreversibility guardrails — requires metacognition about regret and consequence
  • Framing psychometric data for HR teams — requires understanding organisational context AI cannot infer
  • Handling sensitive candidate data that cannot be sent to external AI systems
  • Reading the unspoken business risk in a “simple” feature request
The principle I designed by: Humans over-rely on AI when it removes friction from decisions they should be making themselves. I built that assumption into every AI-adjacent feature — specifically in the AI Resume Parser, where the goal was to ensure employers never became bystanders in their own hiring process.

DEEP DIVE: AI RESUME PARSER

Designing high-stakes automation with human control

The Challenge

Founders wanted automated resume screening integrated with ATS workflows. The risk: over-automation could silently reject qualified candidates, creating hiring mistakes, legal liability, and client churn.

My Approach: Three Options Explored

✕ Rejected

Fully Automatic

AI screens all resumes → auto-advances qualified candidates. Fast but removes human control — too risky for hiring decisions.

✕ Rejected

Manual Review Required

AI extracts data → employers review every candidate. Safe but defeats automation purpose — doesn’t save time.

✓ Chosen

AI-Assisted with Guardrails

AI parses and scores → employers set thresholds → manual override always available. Balanced automation with control — AI does heavy lifting, humans retain final authority.

The Complete 6-Step Flow I Designed

1
ATS Integration Gate

Designed contextual empty states for different system conditions (ATS not connected, no jobs exist, no candidates available, job not configured). Prevented users from entering broken flows.

2
Job Selection & Stage Mapping

Display ATS jobs in “Not Setup” state by default. Employer selects which ATS stage to fetch candidates from. Connected external pipeline data with internal screening logic.

3
AI Criteria Suggestion

Based on role name and job description, AI suggests 10 evaluation criteria. Employer must select minimum 8. Option to create completely custom criteria from scratch. Why: Balanced AI assistance with recruiter judgment (Principle 2: Transparency).

4
Score Threshold Configuration

Employer defines High/Medium/Low bands. Sets automation rules per band (e.g., “Above 85% → auto-advance; Below 40% → auto-reject”). Key decision: Added clear warning — “Auto-rejected candidates cannot be recovered.” Made automation consequences explicit (Principle 4: Guardrails).

5
Parsing Controls

Default candidate processing limit: 20 per batch. Pause/resume capability during active screening. Sync status visibility. Manual re-sync option as fallback. Why: Prevented bulk processing errors, allowed recovery from mistakes.

6
Candidate Evaluation Interface

Under each job, employers see High/Medium/Low badges and criteria-level scoring breakdown. On candidate detail page: resume preview, basic info, manual action controls (Reject, Move stage, Invite to assessment). Why: Full transparency into AI decisions while maintaining human control at all decision points.

Critical Design Decision: Confidence Score Visualization

Challenge: Show AI certainty without overwhelming users.

Solution: Slider with snap points at 50%, 70%, 90%. Colour-coded zones (red <50%, yellow 50–70%, green >70%).

Trade-off: Simplified continuous scores into discrete buckets — but employers needed clear decision points, not granular precision.

Strategic Influence Example

Positioned “Request New Integration” button top-right for easy access. When questioned in team discussion, founder defended my placement decision: “That’s the correct position — we’re going to monetize integration requests.” On click, users see popup for integration name; submission triggers email to sales/CS/Testlify teams who assess feasibility and respond with pricing. This showed I was thinking about business model, not just UI placement.

Honest Outcome: Feature went live during my tenure but had implementation gaps. I identified 15+ issues (edge cases, validation logic, error handling), created a resolution roadmap with design solutions, and handed off to development. This was my biggest, most complex feature — demonstrating founders’ trust in strategic work.

DEEP DIVE: REPORTING AS A PRODUCT

The Problem I Identified
  • Basic reports lacked depth for enterprise HR decision-making
  • Psychometric reports weren’t branded or had UI inconsistencies
  • No unified export system — users downloaded 5–10 separate PDFs per candidate
  • Inconsistent visual language across report types

My Approach: Three Directions Explored

✕ Rejected

Basic Template Redesign

Clean up existing reports; keep separate PDFs. Quick fix but doesn’t solve fragmentation or structural issues.

✕ Rejected

Unified Report Builder

Let employers customise sections, choose which tests to include, design their own layouts. Too complex for most users — over-engineered for the problem.

✓ Chosen

Curated Report System

Design new reports from scratch where needed; fix and improve existing reports; build smart export. Professional output without configuration overhead.

STRATEGIC INFLUENCE & WHAT I LEARNED

How I Shaped Product Direction

Monetisation Thinking
Positioned “Request New Integration” button top-right in Resume Parser. When questioned, founder defended my decision: we’re monetising integration requests, so placement needs visibility. Showed I was thinking about business model, not just interface.
Scope Management
When urgent client/internal requests arrived, I asked founders to reprioritise existing work rather than accepting everything. Learned to frame pushback as business risk: “Shipping this now creates support burden” vs. “I need more time.”
Process Improvement
Proposed we start writing product/feature documentation as standard practice. Project manager rejected due to time constraints, but proposal showed initiative to improve team operations beyond my individual work.

What I’d Do Differently

Push for Structured Requirements Earlier
Reality: I accepted ambiguous requirements and figured things out myself. Impact: Some features solved wrong problems. Library preview addressed “choice paralysis” when real issue was “unclear test quality indicators.” Learning: In next role, I’ll advocate for minimal research/validation before design — even lightweight user interviews prevent building wrong solutions.
Document Design Decisions Better
Reality: Under velocity pressure, I didn’t document rationale for key decisions. Impact: When I left, some design context was lost. New team didn’t know why certain patterns existed, risking regression. Learning: Now I use lightweight decision logs — even 3 bullets captures critical context for future team members.
Quality Gates Without Bottlenecking
Reality: Holding tickets for quality sometimes created delivery delays. Impact: Tension between my quality standards and business urgency. Learning: Quality gates need clear criteria and escalation paths. “Hold until perfect” isn’t sustainable; “hold until safe” is.
