THE REAL CHALLENGE

BUILDING STRUCTURE WHERE NONE EXISTED

Most case studies document how a designer built a feature. This one is about something harder: deciding what to build, why, and what could break — before a single frame was opened.

Founders provided high-level references: “Build something like this competitor feature.” No PRDs. No structured documentation. Requirements arrived through Slack, sales calls, and client tickets — vague, half-defined, and missing edge cases.

The core complexity: One employer toggle could ripple simultaneously into candidate status across multiple assessments, PDF output structure, ATS sync behaviour, shareable link access controls, historical data consistency, and cross-platform differences across web, iOS, and Android. Nothing existed in isolation.
My actual responsibility

This wasn't designing flows. It was acting as the product's decision layer — applying a 5-Why discipline to every brief before opening Figma:

Surface tension My decision responsibility
Ambiguous requirements Convert "make resume screening automatic" into structured logic, edge cases, validation rules, and failure states — with documented trade-offs founders could act on
Cascading dependencies Map downstream effects before designing: what does this toggle break in ATS sync, PDF generation, or white-label config?
Technical feasibility Validate with backend teams on constraints before promising solutions to stakeholders
Competing stakeholder priorities Mediate between founders, sales, and CS — prevent design-by-committee with documented trade-offs and clear rationale
Quality gates Block releases until UX matched spec, cross-browser issues were resolved, and logic was sound — regardless of delivery pressure
Constraints

What I was designing within

Every decision on this platform was shaped by a specific set of constraints. Understanding them is necessary to understand why the design choices were made the way they were.

Constraint How it shaped my work
No design manager I had no senior designer to escalate to when stuck. When I hit a blocker, I went directly to the source: senior engineers for technical constraints, the founder for business constraints, and AI to stress-test alignment across all three. This made me faster at triangulating decisions independently — but it also meant every call was mine to own
Revenue-driven prioritisation There was a standing instruction: revenue-impacting tasks come first. This meant my existing plate was constantly re-stacked. A strategic epic could be paused mid-flow because a client-facing fix needed to ship that sprint. I learned to design for interruptibility — keeping work at a state where it could be picked back up without losing context
Technical constraints from engineers directly There was no PM translating technical feasibility to me. I went directly to senior engineers to understand what was and wasn’t buildable before committing to a design direction. This eliminated the common gap between what design proposes and what engineering can actually deliver
Proactive issue resolution before handoff If I encountered an issue or edge case during the design phase — a logic gap, a missing state, an unhandled error — I resolved it and included the solution in the handoff. Problems were never passed downstream to be discovered in dev or QA

Decision-making framework

Five principles that governed every ticket

With 229 tickets spanning multiple surfaces, ad-hoc decisions compound into inconsistency. These weren’t aspirational values — they were working constraints I enforced on every decision:

01

Control over automation

AI augments, never replaces, human judgment. Employers retain final authority over every AI recommendation.

02

Transparency in complexity

When the system makes decisions — AI scoring, test logic, candidate filtering — show users why and how.

03

Progressive disclosure

Reveal complexity gradually. Essential information first, advanced controls on demand.

04

Guardrails, not gates

Prevent errors through smart defaults and validation — not by blocking user actions outright.

05

Consistency across chaos

Patterns must hold when the platform is expanding at velocity. Documented and enforced, not assumed.

HUMAN JUDGMENT IN AI-DRIVEN PRODUCTS

Where I used AI — and where I didn’t

Working on an AI hiring platform gave me first-hand clarity on where AI genuinely helps — and where it fails in ways that hurt real people. I was deliberate about this boundary at every feature decision.

AI handled well
  • Edge-case stress testing and logic gap identification
  • Generating suggested criteria for resume screening rules
  • Structured data extraction from unformatted resumes
  • Confidence scoring on resume-to-role match
Human judgment was irreplaceable
  • Navigating founder vs. sales vs. user conflicts — requires empathy and political awareness no model has
  • Designing irreversibility guardrails — requires metacognition about what users will regret under pressure
  • Framing psychometric data for senior HR leadership — requires understanding unspoken organisational context
  • Mapping cascading dependency risk — requires holding the entire system in working memory simultaneously
  • Deciding when to push back on a “simple” request — requires reading unspoken business risk behind the ask
  • Bridging context gaps when private enterprise hiring data cannot be shared with external AI tools
The principle I designed by: AI can process. It cannot own a decision. The moment a consequence is irreversible — a candidate rejected, an employer's trust broken, a system's integrity compromised — a human must hold it. I built that assumption into every AI-adjacent feature on this platform.
Deep dive — flagship feature

AI Resume Parser: high-stakes automation with human control

The ask: Build an AI-powered system that screens resumes, extracts candidate data, matches qualifications to job requirements, and routes qualified candidates into assessment workflows — reducing time-to-hire for companies processing hundreds of applications.

The risk: AI making consequential hiring decisions without employer oversight could eject qualified candidates, introduce bias, and erode platform trust — with no way to recover.

Three approaches — one decision
✕ REJECTED

Fully Automatic

AI screens all resumes and auto-advances candidates. Fast — but removes employer control entirely. Too risky for decisions that affect people's careers.

✕ REJECTED

Manual Review Required

AI extracts data; employers review every candidate before advancing. Safe — but defeats the purpose of automation. Doesn't reduce time-to-hire at scale.

✓ CHOSEN

AI-Assisted with Guardrails

AI parses and scores resumes; employers set configurable thresholds and can review or override any decision. AI does the heavy lifting. Humans retain final authority.

The complete flow — 6 decision points
1

ATS Integration

Connect to 100+ ATS platforms (Greenhouse, Lever, Workable). Designed contextual empty states for each prerequisite blocker — ATS not connected, no jobs available, no candidates synced, job not configured — making each blocker actionable, not just an error.

2

Job Stage Selection

Dynamic dropdown populated from the connected ATS's actual stage names — eliminating guessing across different ATS naming conventions. Required complex backend integration, but eliminated an entire class of user confusion.

3

Screening Rules

AI suggests relevant criteria based on role; employers can add custom requirements or override defaults. The AI assists. The employer decides what matters for their specific hire.

4

Threshold Configuration

Slider with snap points at 50%, 70%, 90%. Colour-coded zones — red <50%, yellow 50–70%, green >70%. Trade-off: Simplified continuous AI scores into discrete buckets. Accepted because employers needed clear decision points, not statistical precision.

5

Batch Processing

Processes up to 500 resumes per run. Auto-advance toggle is opt-in and off by default — when disabled, all candidates queue for review. Added configuration complexity to give employers the control that high-stakes decisions demand.

6

Review & Override

Two rejection paths: Auto-reject (removes from ATS) or Keep for manual review. Critical guardrail: Auto-reject carries a persistent, unmissable warning — “Auto-rejected candidates cannot be recovered.” Desired by high-volume recruiters; required an irreversibility signal to prevent bulk rejections made under time pressure.

Critical Design Decision: Confidence Score Visualization

Challenge: Show AI certainty without overwhelming users.

Solution: Slider with snap points at 50%, 70%, 90%. Colour-coded zones (red <50%, yellow 50–70%, green >70%).

Trade-off: Simplified continuous scores into discrete buckets — but employers needed clear decision points, not granular precision.

Strategic Influence Example

Positioned “Request New Integration” button top-right for easy access. When questioned in team discussion, founder defended my placement decision: “That’s the correct position — we’re going to monetize integration requests.” On click, users see popup for integration name; submission triggers email to sales/CS/Testlify teams who assess feasibility and respond with pricing. This showed I was thinking about business model, not just UI placement.

Honest Outcome: Feature went live during my tenure but had implementation gaps. I identified 15+ issues (edge cases, validation logic, error handling), created a resolution roadmap with design solutions, and handed off to development. This was my biggest, most complex feature — demonstrating founders’ trust in strategic work.

DEEP DIVE: REPORTING AS A PRODUCT

The Problem I Identified
  • Basic reports lacked depth for enterprise HR decision-making
  • Psychometric reports weren’t branded or had UI inconsistencies
  • No unified export system — users downloaded 5–10 separate PDFs per candidate
  • Inconsistent visual language across report types

My Approach: Three Directions Explored

✕ Rejected

Basic Template Redesign

Clean up existing reports; keep separate PDFs. Quick fix but doesn’t solve fragmentation or structural issues.

✕ Rejected

Unified Report Builder

Let employers customise sections, choose which tests to include, design their own layouts. Too complex for most users — over-engineered for the problem.

✓ Chosen

Curated Report System

Design new reports from scratch where needed; fix and improve existing reports; build smart export. Professional output without configuration overhead.

STRATEGIC INFLUENCE & WHAT I LEARNED

How I Shaped Product Direction

Monetisation Thinking
Positioned “Request New Integration” button top-right in Resume Parser. When questioned, founder defended my decision: we’re monetising integration requests, so placement needs visibility. Showed I was thinking about business model, not just interface.
Scope Management
When urgent client/internal requests arrived, I asked founders to reprioritise existing work rather than accepting everything. Learned to frame pushback as business risk: “Shipping this now creates support burden” vs. “I need more time.”
Process Improvement
Proposed we start writing product/feature documentation as standard practice. Project manager rejected due to time constraints, but proposal showed initiative to improve team operations beyond my individual work.

What I’d Do Differently

Push for Structured Requirements Earlier
Reality: I accepted ambiguous requirements and figured things out myself. Impact: Some features solved wrong problems. Library preview addressed “choice paralysis” when real issue was “unclear test quality indicators.” Learning: In next role, I’ll advocate for minimal research/validation before design — even lightweight user interviews prevent building wrong solutions.
Document Design Decisions Better
Reality: Under velocity pressure, I didn’t document rationale for key decisions. Impact: When I left, some design context was lost. New team didn’t know why certain patterns existed, risking regression. Learning: Now I use lightweight decision logs — even 3 bullets captures critical context for future team members.
Quality Gates Without Bottlenecking
Reality: Holding tickets for quality sometimes created delivery delays. Impact: Tension between my quality standards and business urgency. Learning: Quality gates need clear criteria and escalation paths. “Hold until perfect” isn’t sustainable; “hold until safe” is.
